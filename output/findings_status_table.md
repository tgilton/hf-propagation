## Summary: thin data vs. unrecognized confound

Across these four claims, **outright data insufficiency was never the primary failure mode** — even the closest case (Kp spot-count, #4) that has a genuine thin-data component (only 5 of 84 days ever reached Severe Kp) was independently confirmed absent via a regression on a properly aggregated, reasonably large n (13,461 cells), not merely "inconclusive for lack of data."

The dominant pattern is **confound-in-plentiful-data**:

- **#1 (solar-noon paradox)** failed inside a 7.19-million-spot pool. The apparent physical effect was an artifact of SFI level being entangled with which specific week got sampled — pooling months together silently combined two different, uncontrolled variables (season and flux) into one axis.
- **#4 (Kp spot-count)** failed for a similar structural reason: an unequal day-count artifact (77 Quiet days vs 5 Severe days) made a naive pooled comparison meaningless regardless of the total spot count — the raw numbers were large, but the comparison itself was invalid before any statistics were applied.
- **#2 (seasonal anomaly)** is the intermediate case: the underlying direction is real and confirmed on a large, well-powered sample, but the reported *magnitude* doesn't survive — and the one data pattern that superficially looked like it could explain the missing magnitude (a wide spread at SZA=80°) was itself another instance of the same failure mode (a thin-sample, non-seasonal comparison being mistaken for a seasonal one).
- **#3 (Kp SNR)** is the control case: it survives cleanly, and precisely because the effect it claims (near-zero difference) doesn't require disentangling anything — there's no direction to be confounded when the true effect size is ~0.

For the paper's argument, this pushes toward the more interesting framing: **three of four claims examined ran into a real, unrecognized confound sitting inside a dataset that was large in aggregate** (millions of spots), not a claim starved of data. "Even 215M spots can mislead if pooled across an uncontrolled variable" is the finding these four checks actually support — plain data scarcity was at most a compounding factor in one case (#4), never the standalone explanation.
