## Summary: thin data vs. unrecognized confound

Across these five claims, **outright data insufficiency was never the primary failure mode** — even the closest case (Kp spot-count, #4) that has a genuine thin-data component (only 5 of 84 days ever reached Severe Kp) was independently confirmed absent via a regression on a properly aggregated, reasonably large n (13,461 cells), not merely "inconclusive for lack of data."

The dominant pattern is **confound-in-plentiful-data**, but check #5 adds a distinct third failure mode:

- **#1 (solar-noon paradox)** failed inside a 7.19-million-spot pool. The apparent physical effect was an artifact of SFI level being entangled with which specific week got sampled — pooling months together silently combined two different, uncontrolled variables (season and flux) into one axis.
- **#4 (Kp spot-count)** failed for a similar structural reason: an unequal day-count artifact (77 Quiet days vs 5 Severe days) made a naive pooled comparison meaningless regardless of the total spot count — the raw numbers were large, but the comparison itself was invalid before any statistics were applied.
- **#2 (seasonal anomaly)** is the intermediate case: the underlying direction is real and confirmed on a large, well-powered sample, but the reported *magnitude* doesn't survive — and the one data pattern that superficially looked like it could explain the missing magnitude (a wide spread at SZA=80°) was itself another instance of the same failure mode (a thin-sample, non-seasonal comparison being mistaken for a seasonal one).
- **#3 (Kp SNR)** is the control case: it survives cleanly, and precisely because the effect it claims (near-zero difference) doesn't require disentangling anything — there's no direction to be confounded when the true effect size is ~0.
- **#5 (Es contamination) introduces a third, distinct failure mode: the claimed pattern simply isn't present in the data at all**, independent of any month/confound framing — no second peak or valley shows up at any SZA cut or bin resolution tested, on either side of a 7.3-million-spot comparison. This isn't a pooling confound like #1/#4, and it isn't a real-but-overstated effect like #2 — the core visual/distributional claim doesn't reproduce, period. Within the same check, the secondary monthly-ranking claim does show the now-familiar confound signature (Es fraction anti-correlated with the sampled week's Kp/F10.7 at r=-0.98/-0.75, though n=4 months is too small to confirm), while the diurnal-timing sub-claim is the most robust finding of the whole check, reproducing cleanly within each of 4 individual months.

For the paper's argument, this pushes toward a three-part framing rather than a single one: (1) **confounds hiding in large pooled datasets** (#1, #4, and partly #2) — "even millions of spots can mislead if pooled across an uncontrolled variable"; (2) **real effects that survive direction but not magnitude** (#2) — the paper's claims about *existence* of an effect are on firmer ground than its claims about *size*; and now (3) **claimed patterns that don't exist in the data at all** (#5) — a reminder that some figures in the original report may reflect a methodological choice, visualization artifact, or notebook cell that no longer survives to check, rather than any of the above. Plain data scarcity remains, at most, a compounding factor (#4) — never the standalone explanation for any of the five.
